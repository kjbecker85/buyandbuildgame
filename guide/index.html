Session guide

Run Buy and Build with a team or a class

This guide is for the person running the session. It covers the setup, a minute by minute plan for 90 minutes, what to watch while people play, and questions for the debrief, the discussion of the results at the end.

What the game is

A private equity fund raises money from investors, buys companies with that money and a bank loan, grows them, and sells them a few years later. In the game, each player runs one such fund, with $50 million from its investors. The player buys one company in Dallas/Fort Worth with a bank loan, then buys smaller companies in the towns around it. Each smaller company is called an add-on. Two rival funds, played by the computer, buy companies on the same map under the same rules.

Each turn is one quarter of a year. For the first three years the player grows the company. From Year 4 to Year 7 buyers make offers, and the player chooses when to sell. After Year 7 the sale is forced.

The score is fund value: the money the fund has not invested, plus what its company would sell for today, less the company's loan. A fund must finish above the investors' line, which grows every dollar invested by 8 percent a year. A fund that ends below the line has lost. Above the line, the three funds are ranked by fund value.

A quick deal is one company and takes about 25 minutes. The full fund is ten years of deals and takes 60 to 90 minutes.

Before the session

Setup takes about ten minutes, plus the time to play one game yourself.

  1. Play one quick deal yourself at buyandbuildgame.com/play. You will see every page your players see.
  2. Make an account. On the title page choose Sign in, type an email and a password, and choose Create an account. No confirmation email is sent.
  3. Make a group. Open Leaderboard, then Groups. Name the group and make it. You get a group code and a link.
  4. Send the link to your players, or put the group code on the screen at the start.

What players need: a laptop, a tablet or a phone with a current browser. There is nothing to install. Each player makes a free account, so that their score reaches the group's leaderboard. Everyone in the group can see each player's name and fund name on that leaderboard.

The same market for everyone. Each new game has its own companies, its own market cycle and its own news, so two players can face very different luck. To give every player the same game, finish a quick deal yourself, choose Share your result, and send that link too. Everyone who opens it gets the same companies, the same market cycle and the same news.

Plan for a 90 minute session

TimeWhat happens
0 to 10Frame it. Private equity means buying a company, making it better and selling it for more. Say the goal: finish above the investors' line, then beat the two rival funds. Do not explain the moves. The game teaches each one at the moment it matters.
10 to 15Join. Players open the link, make an account and join the group. They pick a name, a fund name and a game piece.
15 to 50Play a quick deal. Most players finish in 25 to 35 minutes. Fast players can start a second deal, and their best score counts.
50 to 55Show the leaderboard. Open the group's leaderboard on the big screen. Ask the top two and the bottom two to say what they bought and how much they borrowed.
55 to 85Debrief. Use the questions below. Start with the loan, then the add-ons, then the timing of the sale.
85 to 90Close. Ask each player for one thing they would do differently. For a class, set the full fund as homework.

For a 50 minute class, set the quick deal as homework before class. Use class time for the leaderboard and the debrief.

What to watch while they play

  • The first choice. Three companies are for sale: one well run with a high price, one in the middle, and one cheap one whose owner does everything. Note who picked which, and who paid to check the books for hidden problems before buying.
  • The size of the loan. A bigger loan raises the return when profit grows, and it brings the bank in sooner when profit falls. Players who lose the company to the bank have usually borrowed the most.
  • The management team. Without a management team to run the merger, a company keeps only a little over half of the profit of each add-on it buys. Players who hire the team early pull ahead.
  • Add-ons. They come to market from Year 2 to Year 5, one in each town. Each rival fund says at the start of the year which town it wants, and buys it in the second quarter. Watch who reads that and buys the town first.
  • Call it. In a quiet quarter the game asks the player to predict one number. Players who open the statements before they answer are right more often, and a right call earns career points.
  • The sale. Offers rise and fall with the market. Some players sell at the first offer. Others hold to Year 7 and are forced to sell.

Debrief questions

The end page of each game shows where the gain came from. Ask players to keep it open.

  1. Which company did you buy, and what did you pay? Compare the three choices. Did the cheap company turn out cheap? What did checking the books find?
  2. How much did you borrow? What did the loan do to your return? Did the bank ever step in, and which number warned you first?
  3. Where did your gain come from? The end page splits it into three parts: higher yearly profit, a higher multiple, and a smaller loan. Which was largest for you? Which of those is in your control?
  4. What did you pay for add-ons, and what multiple did you sell at? Add-ons cost about 5 times their yearly profit. A bigger company sells for a higher multiple of its yearly profit. The difference between the two multiples is where buy and build makes its money.
  5. Which moves did you repeat? A move pays best the first time and less the second time. What happened to sales when you raised prices again?
  6. Profit and cash are different things. Where did you see the difference? Look for a busy quarter in which profit rose and cash did not. Why did that happen?
  7. When did you sell, and why then? What did the market do after? What would one more year have added, and what would it have cost in yearly return?
  8. Did you beat the investors' line? The line grows 8 percent a year. Why do investors ask for that before they count a fund as a success?
  9. What would you do differently? One sentence each.

The ideas, in the game's words

Yearly profit (EBITDA)What the company keeps from sales after direct costs and overhead, over the last four quarters. EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. The price, the loan and the sale are all set from it.
MultipleThe price of a company divided by its yearly profit. A bigger company sells at a higher multiple than a small one.
Add-onA smaller company bought and combined with the first company the fund bought, which is called the platform company.
The loan (leverage)The bank lends part of the price, so the fund puts in less of its own money. A gain is then a bigger multiple of the fund's money, and a loss takes a bigger share of it.
The bank's limit (covenant)The loan may not be more than 4 times yearly profit. Over the limit, the player has one year to get back under it, or the bank takes the company.
Times your money (MOIC)The money the fund gets back divided by the money it put in. MOIC stands for multiple on invested capital. 2.0 times means the fund doubled its money.
Yearly return (IRR)The percent the fund's money grew each year. IRR stands for internal rate of return. The same gain in fewer years is a higher yearly return.
Fund valueMoney not invested, plus what the company would sell for today, less its loan. It is the score.
The investors' lineWhat the fund's investors expect: every dollar put to work must grow 8 percent a year.
